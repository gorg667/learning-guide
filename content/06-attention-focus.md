## Attention is the gate

Everything in the preceding chapters converges on one point: learning happens only to what you attend to. Attention determines what enters working memory; working memory is where encoding happens; and, at the level of neurons, attention releases the neuromodulators that permit plasticity. William James put it in 1890: "My experience is what I agree to attend to." A century of research has only sharpened that claim.

This makes attention the most valuable and most abused resource a learner has. The modern information environment is engineered, with great sophistication and enormous budgets, to capture and fragment it. Learning to protect and direct attention is therefore no longer an optional refinement. It is a precondition for the rest of this guide working at all.

## What attention is

Attention is not a single faculty but a set of related functions. Posner and Petersen's influential model distinguishes three networks:

- **Alerting** — maintaining a state of readiness; the difference between drowsy and sharp. Governed by noradrenaline, strongly affected by sleep, time of day, exercise and caffeine.
- **Orienting** — selecting *where* attention goes: to a location, an object, a sensory channel. Can be driven top-down (you choose) or bottom-up (something grabs it — a movement, a sound, your name, a notification).
- **Executive control** — resolving conflict between competing demands, inhibiting distraction, holding a goal in mind, switching between tasks. This is the network most closely tied to working memory's central executive, and the one most depleted by fatigue and overuse.

Two properties matter most for learning.

**Attention is selective and limited.** You cannot attend to two demanding things at once. When it appears that you are, you are either alternating rapidly (with costs at every switch) or one of the tasks is so automatic that it requires almost no attention (walking while talking). Genuinely parallel attention to two streams of new information is not possible.

**Attention has a bottom-up capture system that you do not control.** Sudden onsets, movement, sounds, faces, your own name and anything associated with reward pull attention automatically. This system kept our ancestors alive; it is now the attack surface exploited by every notification and autoplay algorithm. You cannot decide not to be distracted by a notification. You can only decide not to have notifications.

## Sustained attention: the biology of focus

Focus on a single task is not a steady state. Attention naturally fluctuates over seconds and minutes; the mind wanders. Studies using thought-probes find people are mind-wandering roughly 30–50% of the time during ordinary activities, and around 20–40% of the time during lectures and reading — rising steadily as time on task increases. Mind-wandering during reading predicts poor comprehension almost perfectly: what was read while the mind was elsewhere was not encoded.

Sustained attention is effortful and depletes. How long a person can maintain focused attention on demanding material varies enormously — with the task, interest, sleep, practice and individual differences — and the popular figures ("attention span is 8 seconds", "20 minutes") are not supported by solid evidence. What is well supported:

- Attention degrades with continuous time on task (the *vigilance decrement*), and brief breaks restore it.
- Interest and perceived relevance dramatically extend focus. Boredom is a signal that engagement is failing, not a fixed property of the material.
- Sleep deprivation is the single largest destroyer of sustained attention; a night of five hours' sleep produces lapses equivalent to legal intoxication.
- Physical activity, even a short walk, restores attention acutely.
- Attention is trainable. Sustained focused practice — whether in study, meditation or skilled work — increases the ability to sustain focus over months, though it does so slowly and specifically.

## Multitasking: the evidence

### There is no such thing

What people call multitasking is almost always **task-switching**: alternating attention between tasks, with a cost each time. Those costs are well measured:

- **Switch cost.** Every switch requires reconfiguring the mental "task set". Rogers and Monsell (1995) and hundreds of studies since find each switch costs time and accuracy, even for very simple tasks. For complex tasks, the cost is far larger.
- **Attention residue.** Sophie Leroy (2009) showed that after switching away from an unfinished task, part of your attention remains on it, degrading performance on the new task. Checking email "for a second" leaves a residue that persists for minutes.
- **Resumption lag.** After an interruption, returning to a complex task takes substantial time — often several minutes to regain the previous depth of engagement — and error rates rise around the interruption (Altmann, Trafton & Hambrick, 2014, found even 2.8-second interruptions doubled error rates on a sequential task).

Rubinstein, Meyer and Evans (2001) estimated that heavy task-switching can cost up to 40% of productive time. Whether that figure generalises is debatable; that the cost is large and real is not.

### Media multitasking makes you worse at attention, not better

A natural hope is that heavy multitaskers get good at it. The evidence says the opposite. Ophir, Nass and Wagner (2009) found that heavy media multitaskers were *worse* at filtering irrelevant information, worse at task-switching, and more distractible than light multitaskers. Later meta-analyses (Uncapher & Wagner, 2018; Wiradhany & Nieuwenstein, 2017) find the relationships are modest and mostly correlational, but consistently point the same way: heavy media multitasking is associated with poorer sustained attention and more mind-wandering, not with superior parallel processing.

### Multitasking while learning

The laboratory findings are stark when applied to study:

- Students who texted during a lecture scored substantially lower on a subsequent test than those who did not (many studies, e.g., Kuznekoff & Titsworth, 2013: about a grade and a half lower).
- Laptop use for non-class purposes during lectures predicts lower grades, and — importantly — lowers the grades of *neighbours* who can see the screen (Sana, Weston & Cepeda, 2013).
- Divided attention at *encoding* devastates memory; divided attention at *retrieval* is far less harmful (Craik et al., 1996). You can (somewhat) recall while distracted; you cannot learn while distracted.
- Reading with a television on, or with music that has lyrics, measurably reduces comprehension of complex text.

> [!KEY]
> Divided attention during learning is not a mild inefficiency. It removes the material from the encoding process. Ten minutes of fully attended study is worth more than thirty minutes of distracted "study", and the distracted thirty minutes leaves you with a false sense that you have covered the material.

## Phones and notifications

### The mere presence effect — smaller than you've been told

Ward, Duke, Gneezy and Bos (2017) reported that the mere presence of one's own smartphone — face down, silenced, on the desk — reduced working memory capacity and fluid intelligence compared with leaving it in another room. This "brain drain" finding was widely reported. Subsequent meta-analyses (Böttger, Poschik & Zierer, 2023; Hartanto et al., 2024) find the mere-presence effect is **small and inconsistent** across studies; it appears more reliably in people with higher phone attachment or fear of missing out. Putting your phone in another room is still a sensible policy — it costs nothing — but the main damage from phones is not their presence. It is their **use** and their **interruptions**.

### Notifications

A notification you do not act on still costs attention. Stothart, Mitchum and Yehnert (2015) showed that merely receiving a notification — without checking it — impaired performance on an attention task about as much as actively using the phone. Every ping triggers bottom-up orienting and a burst of task-unrelated thought ("who was that? what do they want?"). Given typical rates of dozens to hundreds of notifications a day, the cumulative effect on any sustained cognitive task is severe.

### Blocking mobile internet: a causal test

Castelo et al. (2025, *PNAS Nexus*) ran a randomised trial in which participants blocked mobile internet on their phones for two weeks. Sustained attention improved by an amount the authors compared to being ten years younger; mental health and well-being also improved. This is the strongest causal evidence to date that constant connectivity degrades attention — and that the degradation is reversible.

### Classrooms and PISA

The OECD's PISA 2022 results, covering hundreds of thousands of 15-year-olds, found that about 30% of students reported being distracted by digital devices in most or every maths lesson, and about 65% in at least some. Students who reported being distracted by devices — their own or their classmates' — scored significantly lower in maths, even after controlling for socioeconomic background. Schools with enforced phone restrictions had fewer distracted students. The relationship is correlational but consistent with the experimental evidence.

## The internal distractions

Not all distraction is external. Two internal sources deserve attention.

**Mind-wandering.** As noted, attention drifts on its own. The drift is partly unavoidable and partly a signal — of fatigue, of boredom, of anxiety, of an unresolved concern. Noticing that you've wandered and returning is itself a trainable skill (it is essentially what focused-attention meditation trains). Useful moves: briefly note the intruding thought on paper to deal with later (this reliably reduces its pull), make the task more active so there is less room to drift, or take the wandering as a cue for a short break.

**Worry and rumination.** Anxiety consumes working memory. Sian Beilock's work on choking and maths anxiety shows that worry literally occupies the capacity needed for the task. A simple, well-replicated countermeasure is **expressive writing**: spending ten minutes before a stressful task writing about your worries reduces their intrusion and improves performance (Ramirez & Beilock, 2011). [Chapter 24](24-stress-emotion.md) covers this in depth.

## Background music, noise and where you study

- **Music with lyrics** impairs reading comprehension and verbal memory; the lyrics compete for the phonological loop. The effect is robust.
- **Instrumental music** has mixed effects: for some people and some tasks it is neutral or mildly positive (mood, arousal, masking worse noise); for complex reading and problem-solving it is typically mildly negative. Familiar, low-complexity, non-lyrical music during low-load tasks is the least harmful; silence or steady noise is best for high-load ones.
- **Intermittent speech** — a café conversation you can half-follow, a colleague on the phone — is the worst kind of background noise for verbal work. Steady broadband noise (white/pink/brown noise, rain) masks it and is generally preferable to silence in a noisy environment.
- **Familiar environments with fixed cues** help you start, but varying study locations occasionally builds more context-independent memory ([Chapter 4](04-memory-systems.md)). The compromise: a habitual place for routine sessions, varied places for periodic review.

## Building the capacity for focus

### Design the environment first

Willpower is a terrible strategy against a system designed by thousands of engineers to defeat it. Design your environment so that focus is the default and distraction requires effort:

- **Phone in another room** during study, or in a drawer, in Do Not Disturb, with all non-human notifications permanently disabled. Not "face down on the desk".
- **A single-purpose study setup.** If you study on a computer, use a separate user account or browser profile with no social media logins, a site blocker (Cold Turkey, Freedom, SelfControl, LeechBlock or the built-in focus modes) scheduled for your study hours, and only the tabs you need.
- **Close everything else.** Email, chat and messaging apps are the biggest culprits because they generate interrupts. Check them at scheduled times.
- **Physical cues.** A clear desk, the materials laid out, water, headphones with steady noise if the environment is noisy.
- **Tell people.** Interruptions from humans are the most socially costly to refuse. A visible signal (headphones, a closed door, a shared calendar block) and an agreed norm ("I'm offline 9–11; message me and I'll reply at 11") work better than hoping.

### Work in bounded blocks

The evidence on sustained attention supports working in defined blocks with genuine breaks. The Pomodoro technique (25 minutes work, 5 break) is a reasonable default for people who find starting hard or whose attention is fragile; many experienced learners find longer blocks (50/10, 90/20) suit deep work better. The block length matters less than the rules: **one task, no switching, and a real break** (stand up, look far away, move) rather than a "break" spent on the phone, which resets none of the fatigue and adds attention residue. Chapter 28 covers time structures in detail.

### Practise single-tasking

Attention is a skill and improves with practice — specifically, with practice at noticing you've drifted and returning. Two routes have decent evidence:

- **Focused-attention meditation** — attending to the breath, noticing when attention leaves, returning it — produces measurable improvements in sustained attention and reductions in mind-wandering after a few weeks of daily practice (Mrazek et al., 2013 found two weeks of training improved GRE reading comprehension scores and working memory capacity in students, largely by reducing mind-wandering). The effects are modest and require regular practice, but the practice is free.
- **Deliberately single-tasking ordinary activities** — reading a long article to the end without checking anything, doing one task at a time at work, walking without headphones. Each is a repetition of the return-to-focus movement.

### Manage the alerting system

Focus depends on physiological readiness:

- **Sleep** is non-negotiable ([Chapter 22](22-sleep.md)). No attention technique compensates for a sleep-deprived brain.
- **Time of day.** Most people have peak alertness in the late morning and a dip in the early afternoon; evening peaks are common in adolescents and some adults. Schedule demanding learning in your peak; routine review in the trough.
- **Exercise** — a brisk 20-minute walk improves attention for the following hour or two ([Chapter 23](23-exercise-nutrition.md)).
- **Caffeine** improves alertness and sustained attention in habitual doses; it does not improve learning beyond that, and it disrupts sleep if consumed within about eight hours of bedtime.
- **Hunger and dehydration** degrade attention measurably.

### Make the material demand attention

Passive material — reading, watching — leaves attention free to wander. Active engagement — retrieving, explaining, solving, writing, questioning — occupies it. This is one reason the techniques of Part II work: they leave less room for the mind to leave. If you find yourself drifting while reading, the answer is not to try harder to concentrate on reading; it is to stop reading and start doing something with what you've read.

## Attention and the illusion of productivity

A last warning. The distracted, switching, phone-checking mode of work *feels* busy and produces a sensation of activity. It is easy to spend three hours "studying" in this mode and have done perhaps forty minutes of actual encoding. Because you remember being at the desk for three hours, you conclude that you studied for three hours and that the material is just hard. The gap between time spent and learning achieved is filled with distraction, and it is invisible unless you measure it.

Two ways to measure it: keep a simple tally of how many times you switched away during a block (most people are shocked), or — better — test yourself at the end of every block on what you covered. If you can't retrieve it, you weren't there.

> [!PRACTICE]
> For your next study session: phone in another room; one browser window with only what you need; a site blocker on; a piece of paper for stray thoughts; a timer for a 25- to 50-minute block. At the end, close everything and write down what you learned from memory. Compare the experience — and the recall — with your usual session. Then make the setup your default.

You now have the foundations: what learning is, how the brain changes, how memory is structured and limited, how to manage its load, and how to protect the attention that feeds it. The last foundational chapter confronts the most counterintuitive finding of all — that the conditions which make learning *feel* hard are frequently the ones that make it *work*.
