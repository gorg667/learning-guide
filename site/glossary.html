Chapter 44Glossary

Definitions of the technical terms used throughout the guide, with pointers to the chapters where each is explained.

2,295 words · 10 min read

Advance organiser — A brief overview of the structure of material presented before the material itself, giving details something to attach to. (Ch. 4, 16)

Attention residue — The persistence of attention on a previous task after switching to a new one, degrading performance on the new task. (Ch. 6)

Autonomous stage — The final stage of skill acquisition in which performance is fast, accurate and largely unconscious; also where improvement stalls unless deliberately reactivated. (Ch. 18, 33)

BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor) — A protein that supports synaptic plasticity and neuron survival; raised by aerobic exercise. (Ch. 3, 23)

Blocked practice — Practising one topic or skill repeatedly before moving to the next (AAA BBB CCC). Produces good performance during practice and poor retention and discrimination. Contrast interleaving. (Ch. 10)

Body doubling — Working in the presence of another person who is also working; improves task initiation and focus, especially with ADHD. (Ch. 37, 38)

Brain dump — Free recall: writing everything you remember about a topic with the materials closed, then checking. The simplest form of retrieval practice. (Ch. 8)

Calibration — The match between confidence and accuracy in judging one's own knowledge. (Ch. 13)

Chunk — A group of elements bound into a single unit in long-term memory, occupying one slot in working memory. Expertise consists largely of a vast library of chunks. (Ch. 4)

Cognitive load — The demand placed on working memory during learning; intrinsic (inherent to the material), extraneous (from poor design or distraction) and germane (productive schema-building). (Ch. 5)

Complementary learning systems — The theory that the hippocampus learns fast and stores episodes while the neocortex learns slowly and extracts structure, with sleep-dependent transfer between them. (Ch. 3)

Concept map — A network diagram of concepts (nodes) connected by labelled relationships. Contrast mind map. (Ch. 12)

Concreteness fading — Progressing from concrete examples to abstract representations of a concept. (Ch. 11)

Consolidation — The stabilisation and reorganisation of a memory after encoding; synaptic (hours) and systems (days to years); largely sleep-dependent. (Ch. 2, 22)

Contextual interference — The motor-learning term for the interleaving effect: random practice order impairs acquisition and improves retention and transfer. (Ch. 33)

Cornell method — A note-taking layout with a cue column, a notes column and a summary, designed to convert notes into retrieval prompts. (Ch. 15)

Deliberate practice — Effortful, goal-directed practice at the edge of ability, targeting weaknesses, with immediate feedback and repetition with refinement. Contrast naive practice. (Ch. 18)

Desirable difficulty — A condition that makes learning harder in the moment but improves long-term retention and transfer: spacing, interleaving, retrieval, generation, variation. (Ch. 7)

Dual coding — Representing information in both verbal and visual form, producing two memory traces and using both channels of working memory. Not the same as "learning styles". (Ch. 12)

Effect size (d, g) — A standardised measure of the size of a difference between groups, in standard deviations. Roughly: 0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large. (Ch. 1)

Elaboration — Adding to, connecting and explaining new information in terms of existing knowledge. (Ch. 11)

Elaborative interrogation — Asking "why is this true?" of a fact and answering from one's own knowledge. (Ch. 11)

Element interactivity — The number of elements that must be held in working memory simultaneously to understand something; the main determinant of intrinsic load. (Ch. 5)

Encoding — The initial registration of an experience into memory. (Ch. 2)

Encoding specificity — The principle that a retrieval cue is effective to the extent it was present at encoding. (Ch. 4)

Episodic memory — Memory for personally experienced events, embedded in time and place. (Ch. 2)

Expertise reversal effect — Instructional techniques that help novices (worked examples, heavy guidance) become unhelpful or harmful for more expert learners. (Ch. 5)

Expressive writing — Writing about one's worries for about ten minutes before a stressful task; reduces the working-memory cost of anxiety. (Ch. 24)

External focus — Attending to the effect of a movement (the target, the sound) rather than the body; improves motor learning. (Ch. 33)

Fading — Gradually removing scaffolding — for example, worked examples with progressively more steps left for the learner. (Ch. 5, 12)

Feynman technique — Explaining a concept in plain language from memory, identifying gaps, and refining. (Ch. 20)

Flow — A state of absorbed, effortless concentration with merged action and awareness, produced by clear goals, immediate feedback and matched challenge and skill. (Ch. 29)

Fluency illusion — Mistaking ease of processing (from recency, familiarity, the presence of the answer) for knowledge. (Ch. 7)

Forgetting curve — Ebbinghaus's finding that retention drops rapidly at first and then more slowly; each successful relearning flattens it. (Ch. 2)

FSRS — Free Spaced Repetition Scheduler; a machine-learning scheduling algorithm modelling difficulty, stability and retrievability; built into Anki. (Ch. 9)

Generation effect — Information produced by the learner is remembered better than information received. (Ch. 11)

Growth mindset — The belief that abilities can be developed through effort and strategy. Interventions have small average effects, concentrated in struggling students. (Ch. 26)

Guidance hypothesis — In motor learning, feedback after every attempt aids performance but can impair retention by preventing self-evaluation. (Ch. 33)

Habit stacking — Attaching a new behaviour to an existing routine that serves as its cue. (Ch. 27)

Hippocampus — The medial temporal lobe structure essential for forming new declarative memories and for spatial memory. (Ch. 3)

Hypercorrection effect — Errors made with high confidence are better remembered after correction than low-confidence errors. (Ch. 7)

Implementation intention — A plan of the form "When X, I will do Y", which roughly doubles follow-through on effortful goals. (Ch. 25, 27)

Incubation — Improvement in problem solving after a break from the problem. (Ch. 20)

Inert knowledge — Knowledge that is possessed but not applied outside the context in which it was learned. (Ch. 19)

Interference — Forgetting caused by competition between memories; retroactive (new disrupts old) and proactive (old disrupts new). (Ch. 4)

Interleaving — Mixing different topics or problem types within a practice session (ABCBCA). Improves discrimination, retention and transfer. (Ch. 10)

Intrinsic / extrinsic motivation — Doing something for its own sake versus for a separable outcome. (Ch. 25)

Judgment of learning (JOL) — A learner's prediction of future recall; accurate when delayed and retrieval-based, inflated when immediate and fluency-based. (Ch. 13)

Keyword method — A vocabulary mnemonic linking a foreign word to a similar-sounding known word through an image. (Ch. 17)

Lag effect — Longer gaps between study sessions generally produce better long-term retention, up to a point dependent on the retention interval. (Ch. 9)

Learning vs performance — The distinction between relatively permanent change (learning) and what one can do right now (performance); the two can move in opposite directions. (Ch. 2, 7)

Learning styles — The (unsupported) claim that matching instruction to a learner's preferred modality improves learning. (Ch. 14)

Long-term potentiation (LTP) — The strengthening of a synapse by repeated activation; the cellular basis of learning. (Ch. 3)

Major system — A mnemonic code mapping digits to consonant sounds so numbers can be turned into words. (Ch. 17)

Mastery goal / performance goal — Aiming to understand and improve versus aiming to demonstrate ability relative to others. (Ch. 25)

Mental model — A general explanatory pattern or principle applicable across domains. (Ch. 19)

Metacognition — Knowledge of and control over one's own cognitive processes; monitoring and regulation of learning. (Ch. 13)

Method of loci (memory palace) — A mnemonic placing vivid images of items at locations along a familiar route. (Ch. 17)

Mind map — A radial diagram of topics and subtopics around a central idea. Less demanding than a concept map. (Ch. 12)

Myelin — The insulating sheath around axons that speeds conduction; increases with use of a pathway. (Ch. 3)

Neuroplasticity — The brain's capacity to change its structure and connections in response to experience, throughout life. (Ch. 3)

Notional machine — A learner's mental model of what the computer does when a program runs. (Ch. 32)

OK plateau — The level at which a skill becomes automatic and "good enough" and stops improving. (Ch. 18)

Parsons problem — A programming exercise in which scrambled lines of a correct program are reassembled. (Ch. 32)

Peer instruction — A teaching method in which students answer a conceptual question, discuss with peers, and answer again. (Ch. 37)

Pomodoro technique — Working in timed blocks (classically 25 minutes) with short breaks. (Ch. 28)

Pretesting / prequestion effect — Attempting questions before studying improves learning of the material, even when the attempts fail. (Ch. 7, 8)

Procedural memory — Memory for skills and habits; learned slowly through practice, largely unconscious, highly durable. (Ch. 2)

Productive failure — Attempting to solve problems before receiving instruction, which improves conceptual understanding and transfer from the subsequent instruction. (Ch. 5, 7)

Protégé effect — Learning more when learning in order to teach. (Ch. 20)

Redundancy effect — Presenting the same information simultaneously in two forms (e.g., text read aloud) impairs learning. (Ch. 5)

Retrieval practice (testing effect) — Recalling information from memory strengthens it more than restudying it. (Ch. 8)

Retrieval strength / storage strength — How accessible a memory is right now versus how well learned it is; gains in storage strength are greatest when retrieval strength is low. (Ch. 4)

Schema — An organised knowledge structure that makes new information meaningful and can be handled as a single unit in working memory. (Ch. 4)

Self-determination theory — The theory that motivation depends on satisfaction of three needs: autonomy, competence and relatedness. (Ch. 25)

Self-efficacy — Belief in one's ability to succeed at a specific task; a strong predictor of achievement, built mainly by mastery experiences. (Ch. 26)

Self-explanation — Explaining to oneself the meaning and rationale of material or of steps in a worked example. (Ch. 11)

Semantic memory — General knowledge stripped of the context in which it was learned. (Ch. 2)

Slow-wave sleep — Deep NREM sleep, concentrated early in the night, most important for consolidating declarative memory. (Ch. 22)

Spacing effect — Study distributed over time produces better retention than the same amount massed. (Ch. 9)

Split-attention effect — Separating information that must be integrated (a diagram and its explanation) impairs learning. (Ch. 5)

SQ3R — Survey, Question, Read, Recite, Review: a structured active-reading method. (Ch. 16)

Successive relearning — Retrieval practice to a criterion, repeated in several spaced sessions. (Ch. 8, 9)

Targeted memory reactivation (TMR) — Presenting cues during sleep that were associated with learning, to strengthen those memories. (Ch. 22)

Transfer — Applying knowledge or skill learned in one context to a different one; near (similar contexts) or far (dissimilar). (Ch. 19)

Transient information effect — Spoken or animated information, once presented, is gone; long segments overload working memory. (Ch. 5)

Undermining effect — Extrinsic rewards for an intrinsically motivated activity can reduce intrinsic motivation. (Ch. 25)

Variable practice — Practising a skill across a range of conditions to build generalisable knowledge. (Ch. 10, 33)

Worked example — A problem presented with its full step-by-step solution; more effective than problem solving for novices when self-explained. (Ch. 5, 12)

Working memory — The limited-capacity system (about four chunks, a few seconds) in which conscious thinking occurs. (Ch. 4)

Zettelkasten — A note-taking system of atomic, linked, permanent notes in one's own words. (Ch. 15)

Zone of proximal development — The range of tasks a learner can do with support but not yet alone; where learning happens. (Ch. 29)
