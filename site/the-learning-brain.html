Chapter 3The brain that learns — neuroplasticity and what actually changes

What physically happens in your brain when you learn — synapses, myelin, the hippocampus and neocortex — how plasticity changes across the lifespan, and what the neuroscience does and does not tell you about how to study.

2,797 words · 12 min read

Why bother with the biology?#

You can be an excellent learner without knowing a single neuron from another. But a working picture of what the brain is doing helps in three ways. It makes the behavioural findings in the rest of this guide make sense rather than seeming like arbitrary rules. It inoculates you against neuro-nonsense — "left-brained learners", "you only use 10% of your brain", "this app rewires your brain in 7 days". And, most importantly, it settles the question that quietly undermines so many adult learners: is it too late for me? The answer from neuroscience is unambiguous, and it is no.

This chapter is deliberately selective. It covers the handful of mechanisms that bear directly on how you should learn.

Learning is physical change#

Every time you learn something, your brain changes physically. This is not a metaphor. Learning leaves a trace — what the neuroscientist Richard Semon in 1904 called an engram — in the pattern and strength of connections between neurons.

The human brain contains roughly 86 billion neurons, each connected to thousands of others at junctions called synapses, giving on the order of a hundred trillion connections. Information is represented not in individual neurons but in patterns of activity across populations of them, and knowledge is stored in the weights of the connections between them: how strongly the firing of one neuron tends to drive the firing of the next.

Synaptic plasticity: cells that fire together wire together#

In 1949 Donald Hebb proposed that when one neuron repeatedly helps to fire another, the connection between them strengthens. This "Hebbian" principle was confirmed in 1973 when Bliss and Lømo discovered long-term potentiation (LTP) in the rabbit hippocampus: brief, high-frequency stimulation of a pathway left it more responsive for hours or days afterwards. Its mirror image, long-term depression (LTD), weakens connections that are not usefully co-active.

The molecular story has been worked out in considerable detail — glutamate binding NMDA receptors, calcium influx, insertion of AMPA receptors into the synapse, and, for lasting changes, gene expression and protein synthesis that physically enlarge the synapse and grow new dendritic spines. Eric Kandel received the Nobel Prize in 2000 largely for showing, in the sea slug Aplysia, that short-term memory involves modifying existing synapses while long-term memory requires building new synaptic connections through protein synthesis.

Two facts from this work matter directly for learners:

Long-term change needs time and repetition spaced out. In Kandel's slugs, and in every system studied since, a single training pulse produces short-lived potentiation; repeated pulses spaced apart trigger the protein synthesis that makes the change last. Massed pulses (all at once) are far less effective than the same number spaced out. The spacing effect that psychologists found behaviourally in the 1880s has a molecular signature.

Plasticity is bidirectional and competitive. Connections that are not used weaken. The brain is not a hard drive that stores everything indefinitely; it is a dynamic system that constantly reallocates resources towards what is being used. "Use it or lose it" is literally true at the synaptic level. This is one reason knowledge that is never retrieved fades — and why retrieving it keeps it.

Structural plasticity: the brain rebuilds itself#

Beyond adjusting synaptic weights, the brain physically remodels itself with learning:

  • Dendritic spines — the tiny protrusions that receive synaptic input — grow, shrink, appear and disappear over hours and days. Learning a motor skill in mice produces new spines within an hour of training; the spines that persist correlate with how well the skill is retained.
  • Grey matter volume changes measurably in humans. Draganski et al. (2004) had volunteers learn to juggle for three months; MRI showed expansion in visual-motion areas, which partially receded when they stopped practising. Medical students studying for exams showed grey matter increases in parietal cortex and hippocampus. London taxi drivers, who must memorise the city's 25,000 streets, have larger posterior hippocampi than controls, and the size scales with years on the job (Maguire et al., 2000; Woollett & Maguire, 2011 showed the change happened during training, so it's not just selection).
  • Adult neurogenesis — the birth of new neurons — continues in the adult hippocampus in rodents, and probably in humans, though the human evidence is contested. Exercise robustly increases it in animals.

Myelin: the speed of thought#

Neurons communicate through electrical signals along axons, and many axons are wrapped in myelin — a fatty insulating sheath produced by cells called oligodendrocytes — which can speed conduction by up to a hundredfold. Since the 2000s it has become clear that myelination is activity-dependent: pathways that are used more get more heavily myelinated (Fields, 2008; McKenzie et al., 2014 showed that blocking new myelin formation in mice prevented them learning a complex motor skill).

This is the physiological basis of a familiar experience: with practice, things that required slow, effortful, conscious step-by-step processing become fast, fluid and automatic. Myelination is slow — weeks to months — and depends on repeated, precise firing of the same circuits. Sloppy, inattentive repetition myelinates sloppy circuits. This is why deliberate practice — attentive, error-correcting, focused on the hard parts — matters far more than mere hours logged (Chapter 18).

Note

Popular accounts sometimes present myelin as "the" secret of skill (e.g., The Talent Code). It is one important mechanism among several — synaptic changes, spine dynamics and network reorganisation all contribute. The practical implications are the same either way: skills are built by many repetitions of accurate, attentive practice spread over time.

The hippocampus and the neocortex: two learning systems#

Why do we need sleep to remember? Why does new knowledge take time to become integrated? Why can you learn a fact in one exposure but need months to become fluent at a skill? The answer to all three lies in the division of labour between two brain systems.

In 1953 a patient known as H.M. had most of both hippocampi removed to treat epilepsy. He lost the ability to form new declarative memories entirely — he could hold a conversation but would forget it within minutes — while his memories from years before the surgery, his intelligence, his language and his ability to learn new motor skills (which he had no recollection of practising) were preserved. H.M. showed that the hippocampus is necessary for forming new declarative memories but not for storing old ones, and that skill learning uses a different system.

Complementary learning systems#

The modern account, formalised by McClelland, McNaughton and O'Reilly in 1995, is the complementary learning systems theory:

  • The hippocampus is a fast learner. It can bind together the elements of a single experience — the place, the people, the words, the feeling — into a unified trace after one exposure. But it has limited capacity, its traces are relatively separate from each other, and they decay.
  • The neocortex is a slow learner. It extracts statistical regularities across many experiences and stores knowledge as overlapping, distributed representations — schemas, concepts, skills. It changes gradually, which protects existing knowledge from being overwritten by each new experience ("catastrophic interference").

The two work together. During the day, the hippocampus rapidly records experiences. During sleep — particularly slow-wave sleep — it replays those experiences, in compressed form, to the neocortex, which gradually integrates them into its existing knowledge structures. Over days, weeks and years, the memory becomes progressively more dependent on cortex and less on hippocampus (systems consolidation), and in the process it becomes more abstract, more connected to other knowledge, and less tied to the original episode.

This architecture explains a great deal:

  • Why sleep is non-negotiable for learning. Replay and cortical integration happen mostly in sleep. Deprive the brain of sleep after learning and the hippocampal traces are never properly transferred (Chapter 22).
  • Why spacing works. Each spaced review gives the cortex another pass at integrating the material with a slightly different hippocampal context, producing a richer, more generalisable representation.
  • Why understanding deepens over time even without further study. Integration into cortical schemas continues for weeks.
  • Why prior knowledge accelerates learning. New information that fits an existing cortical schema can be integrated much faster — Tse et al. (2007) showed rats with a well-established schema could consolidate new related information in 48 hours instead of weeks.
  • Why interleaving works. Presenting different but related items in mixed order is closer to how the neocortex learns best: extracting what is common and what distinguishes items across varied experiences.

The basal ganglia and cerebellum: the skill system#

Procedural learning — habits, motor skills, cognitive routines like mental arithmetic — depends on the basal ganglia (a set of subcortical structures that learn from reward signals to select actions) and the cerebellum (which fine-tunes movements and timing using error signals). Both learn incrementally, from repetition and feedback, and both are largely independent of the hippocampus, which is why H.M. could learn mirror-drawing.

The basal ganglia learn through dopamine, released when an outcome is better than predicted. This "reward prediction error" signal is what makes reinforcement learning work — and what makes reward, surprise and feedback such powerful modulators of learning in general.

Attention, dopamine, and the gates of plasticity#

The brain does not change in response to everything it experiences. Plasticity is gated by neuromodulators — chemicals released broadly across the cortex that signal "this matters, change now":

  • Acetylcholine, released during focused attention, enhances plasticity in the attended circuits and suppresses it elsewhere. In a landmark study, Kilgard and Merzenich (1998) showed that pairing a tone with stimulation of the cholinergic system produced massive reorganisation of auditory cortex, whereas the tone alone did nothing. Attention is not just a filter on input; it is the switch that turns plasticity on. This is the neural reason that passive exposure teaches so little and that distraction during study is so costly.
  • Dopamine signals reward, novelty and prediction error, and enhances the consolidation of what preceded it. Curiosity — a dopaminergic state — measurably improves memory for information encountered while curious, and even for unrelated information encountered at the same time (Gruber, Gelman & Ranganath, 2014).
  • Noradrenaline, released under arousal and stress, sharpens attention and, via the amygdala, strengthens memory for emotionally significant events — up to a point. Beyond that point, chronic or excessive stress impairs the hippocampus (Chapter 24).

The upshot is that the state you are in while learning is not incidental. Attentive, curious, moderately aroused learning is neurologically different from bored, distracted, passive exposure. It literally opens different molecular gates.

Plasticity across the lifespan: is it too late?#

The critical-period myth#

Children's brains are indeed extraordinarily plastic. There are genuine sensitive periods — windows during which certain kinds of learning are dramatically easier: phoneme discrimination in the first year, native-like accent in a second language before roughly puberty, binocular vision in early childhood. The mechanisms are known: during sensitive periods, inhibitory circuits are immature, and the extracellular "brakes" on plasticity (perineuronal nets, certain myelin-associated proteins) have not yet formed.

But the popular extrapolation — that the adult brain is fixed, that you can't learn languages or instruments or new careers after 25 — is false, and the evidence against it is overwhelming:

  • Adults acquire new skills, new languages, new expertise routinely. Adult second-language learners rarely achieve a native accent, but reach full communicative fluency and native-like grammar with sufficient input and practice. A large 2018 study by Hartshorne, Tenenbaum and Pinker found grammar-learning ability stays high until about 17–18 and then declines gradually, not abruptly.
  • The juggling, taxi-driver and medical-student MRI studies above were all in adults; the juggling study was replicated in adults over 60 (Boyke et al., 2008).
  • Stroke rehabilitation depends entirely on adult plasticity and can produce dramatic recovery years after injury.
  • Merzenich and colleagues have shown that intensive, attentive training remodels adult sensory cortex much as it does in juveniles — the difference is that in adults it requires attention and engagement to open the gates, whereas the juvenile brain changes in response to mere exposure.

That last point is the key to adult learning. Children learn from immersion because their plasticity is always on. Adults must earn plasticity by paying attention, being engaged, making effort and caring about the outcome. Passive exposure — having a language playing in the background, sitting in a lecture half-listening — does almost nothing for an adult brain. Focused, effortful, motivated practice does a great deal.

What genuinely changes with age#

Honesty requires noting the real changes:

  • Processing speed slows gradually from the twenties onward.
  • Working memory capacity and the ability to filter distraction decline modestly, especially after 60.
  • Episodic memory — new facts and events — becomes harder to form; older adults need more repetitions and benefit even more from retrieval practice and spacing.
  • Sleep architecture changes: less slow-wave sleep, which is one reason consolidation weakens.

Against these, crystallised knowledge — vocabulary, expertise, accumulated schemas — keeps growing into the seventies and beyond, and because prior knowledge is the biggest accelerator of new learning, older learners in a familiar domain often out-learn younger novices. Learning in an unfamiliar domain is slower and needs more repetition, more structure, more sleep and more patience — but it works. Chapter 36 covers this in depth.

Neuromyths: what the biology does not say#

Neuroscience is routinely hijacked to sell educational products. The following are false, and knowing why helps you spot the next one:

MythReality
"We only use 10% of our brains."Imaging shows activity throughout the brain; damage almost anywhere produces deficits.
"Left-brained / right-brained learners."Both hemispheres are involved in nearly every task; there is no evidence people fall into hemisphere-dominant types (Nielsen et al., 2013, n>1,000).
"Learning styles: visual, auditory, kinaesthetic."People have preferences, but matching instruction to preferred style does not improve learning (Pashler et al., 2008; many replications since). Everyone learns best from the modality that suits the content.
"Brain training games raise intelligence."They improve the trained task; transfer to intelligence or academic outcomes is essentially zero in meta-analyses (Melby-Lervåg et al., 2016; Sala & Gobet, 2019).
"Everything is decided by age 3 / 7 / 25."Sensitive periods exist for a few specific abilities; general learning capacity remains substantial for life.
"Multitasking trains the brain to do more."Heavy media multitaskers are worse at filtering irrelevant information (Ophir, Nass & Wagner, 2009).
"Listening to Mozart makes you smarter."The original effect was a brief arousal effect on one spatial task; it does not raise intelligence.
"Brain Gym / specific exercises activate specific brain regions for learning."No credible evidence; exercise in general benefits cognition, specific movements do not target specific academic skills.

A useful heuristic: if a claim invokes a brain region or a neurotransmitter to sell you a product, and the behavioural evidence for the product is thin, the neuroscience is decoration.

What the biology tells you to do#

Distilled, the neuroscience of learning points to the same conclusions as the behavioural research — which is exactly what you would hope for:

  1. Pay full attention. Attention gates plasticity. Divided attention closes the gate. There is no biological route to learning while distracted.
  2. Make it effortful and active. Passive exposure produces little synaptic change in adult brains; effort, generation and retrieval produce a lot.
  3. Space it out. Lasting synaptic change requires spaced signals. Massed repetition triggers short-term potentiation that fades.
  4. Repeat accurately. Myelination and synaptic strengthening consolidate whatever is being repeated, including errors. Practise the right thing, carefully.
  5. Sleep. Consolidation from hippocampus to cortex happens in sleep. Skimping on sleep throws away a large fraction of the day's learning.
  6. Care about it. Curiosity, meaning and reward release the neuromodulators that mark experiences for retention.
  7. Connect to what you know. Existing cortical schemas dramatically accelerate the integration of new information.
  8. Move. Exercise raises BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor — "fertiliser for neurons"), boosts hippocampal neurogenesis in animals, and improves memory and attention acutely and chronically (Chapter 23).
  9. Don't believe it's too late. It isn't. Adult plasticity is real; it merely demands engagement instead of granting itself for free.
Try this

Notice your state the next time you sit down to learn. Are you attentive, curious and engaged — or half-present, scrolling between paragraphs, "getting through it"? The biology says these are not two intensities of the same activity but two different activities, only one of which builds anything. If you're in the second state, it is better to stop, do something else, and return when you can actually pay attention.
