Chapter 2What learning actually is

A precise definition of learning, the three stages every memory passes through (encoding, consolidation, retrieval), the different kinds of memory you possess, and why "I understood it in the lecture" is not the same as having learned it.

2,795 words · 12 min read

A definition worth memorising#

Ask most people what learning is and they will say something like "taking in information". That definition is the root of most bad study habits, because it suggests the job is to expose yourself to material — read it, watch it, listen to it — and let it soak in.

A better definition, and the one this guide uses throughout:

Learning is a relatively permanent change in knowledge or behaviour, produced by experience, that can be demonstrated later, in a different context, without the original material present.

Every clause is doing work.

  • Relatively permanent. If it's gone next week, you didn't learn it; you temporarily held it. Cramming produces impressive short-term performance and almost no learning in this sense.
  • Change in knowledge or behaviour. Learning is measured by what you can now do or recall that you couldn't before — not by how familiar the material feels.
  • Can be demonstrated later. Learning is only observable through retrieval. This is why testing yourself is not merely a way of measuring learning; as we will see, it is one of the most powerful ways of producing it.
  • In a different context. Knowledge that only works in the exact situation where you acquired it — the same page of the same book, the same room, the same worked example — is brittle. Real learning transfers.
  • Without the original material present. Recognising the right answer when you see it is much easier than producing it. Understanding an explanation while it's in front of you is not the same as being able to reconstruct it.

Hold this definition against your current study habits and most of them will fail at least one clause. That is the beginning of learning how to learn.

Learning versus performance#

Robert Bjork and Elizabeth Ligon Bjork drew a distinction that is arguably the single most important idea in the science of learning: learning is not the same as performance.

Performance is what you can do right now, during or immediately after practice. Learning is the relatively permanent change that underlies what you'll be able to do next month.

The two are not just different — they can move in opposite directions. Conditions that boost performance during practice (massed repetition, predictable order, immediate hints, seeing the answer right away) often reduce long-term learning. Conditions that hurt performance during practice (spacing, mixing topics, having to struggle before getting the answer) often improve it. The Bjorks called these conditions "desirable difficulties", and Chapter 7 is devoted to them.

The practical problem is that you can only feel performance. You cannot feel learning directly; you can only infer it. So learners systematically choose the conditions that make performance high and feel good, and systematically avoid the ones that produce learning. A large part of this guide is about overriding that instinct.

What the research says

In a classic study by Baddeley and Longman (1978), British postal workers learned to type postcodes under different schedules. Those who practised in one-hour sessions, once a day, needed fewer total hours to reach criterion than those doing two two-hour sessions a day — but the intensive group felt they were learning faster and were more satisfied with their schedule. Feeling good and learning well were negatively correlated. This pattern has been replicated hundreds of times since.

The three stages of memory#

Every piece of knowledge you possess has passed through three stages, and each stage has its own failure modes and its own levers.

1. Encoding#

Encoding is the initial registration of an experience into a memory trace. It happens in the moment of attention. Several things determine how strong and how useful the initial trace is:

  • Attention. Nothing that you don't attend to gets encoded in any useful form. Divided attention at encoding is catastrophic: in one line of experiments, people who studied word lists while doing a secondary task recalled about a third less, whereas divided attention at retrieval had a much smaller effect. Encoding needs your full attention; you cannot multitask it.
  • Depth of processing. Fergus Craik and Robert Lockhart showed in the 1970s that what you do with information determines how well you remember it. Processing a word for its meaning (is it a type of animal? does it fit this sentence?) produces far better memory than processing its sound or appearance (does it rhyme? is it in capitals?). The lesson: shallow exposure encodes poorly; thinking about meaning, relationships and implications encodes well.
  • Connection to prior knowledge. New information that hooks onto an existing framework — a schema — is encoded richly and retrieved easily. Information with nothing to attach to is encoded as an isolated fragment and lost quickly. This is why experts learn new material in their field so much faster than novices: they have more hooks.
  • Generation. Producing something yourself — an answer, an example, an explanation — encodes it far better than reading the same thing. Even guessing wrong and then being corrected produces better memory than being told the answer in the first place.
  • Distinctiveness and emotion. Unusual, surprising, or emotionally salient events are encoded more strongly (the amygdala modulates hippocampal encoding). This is the grain of truth behind memory techniques that use vivid imagery.

2. Consolidation#

A freshly encoded memory is fragile. Over the following hours, days and weeks, it is stabilised and reorganised in a process called consolidation, which happens largely without your awareness — and largely during sleep.

There are two levels:

  • Synaptic consolidation (minutes to hours): the molecular and structural changes at synapses that make a trace persist — protein synthesis, receptor changes, growth of dendritic spines.
  • Systems consolidation (days to years): the gradual transfer of dependence from the hippocampus, which rapidly binds the elements of a new experience, to the neocortex, where the memory is integrated with existing knowledge and becomes more abstract, more schematic, and less tied to the original context. Sleep, especially slow-wave sleep, is when hippocampal "replay" drives this process.

Consolidation has three enormous practical consequences:

  1. What you do after learning matters. Sleep deprivation the night after learning can wipe out much of what you encoded. Cramming the night before an exam and sleeping four hours is a strategy for forgetting.
  2. Interference. New learning shortly after old learning can disrupt the consolidation of the earlier material, especially if it's similar. This is one of several reasons to space learning out.
  3. Time is an ingredient. Some kinds of insight — seeing the structure in a domain, noticing the connections — literally cannot be rushed, because they emerge from systems consolidation. You know more about what you studied last month than you did the day after you studied it, in a sense: the knowledge is more integrated even though the details have faded.

3. Retrieval#

Retrieval is the act of accessing a stored memory. Three facts about retrieval reorganise everything about how you should study.

Retrieval is reconstructive, not reproductive. Memory is not a video recording. Each time you recall something, you rebuild it from fragments and from your general knowledge — which is why memories are malleable and why eyewitness testimony is unreliable. But it is also why retrieval is so powerful for learning: reconstructing a memory strengthens and reorganises it.

Retrieval modifies the memory. Successfully retrieving something makes it more retrievable in future — considerably more so than restudying it. This is the testing effect, one of the most robust findings in all of psychology. The harder the retrieval (within limits), the greater the benefit.

Retrieval is cue-dependent. You do not "have" a memory in a simple sense; you have a memory that can be triggered by particular cues. Context, mood, physical state and the exact wording of a question all act as cues. If you always study in the same way with the same cues, your knowledge becomes tied to those cues — available when they are present and inaccessible when they are not. Varying the conditions of practice builds knowledge that can be retrieved under many conditions.

Key idea

Encoding gets information in. Consolidation stabilises it, mostly while you sleep. Retrieval gets it out — and every retrieval strengthens it. Most "study" concentrates entirely on the first stage and treats the third as a mere test of whether the first worked. Effective learning shifts effort towards retrieval, protects consolidation, and only worries about encoding insofar as it makes later retrieval possible.

Kinds of memory (and why they need different training)#

"Memory" is not one thing. The brain has several distinct memory systems, supported by different structures, learned in different ways, and lost separately in different kinds of brain damage. Studying vocabulary the way you would practise a tennis serve is a category error — and so is the reverse.

Declarative (explicit) memory — knowing that#

Declarative memory is what you can consciously recall and state. It depends heavily on the hippocampus and surrounding medial temporal lobe structures for its formation. It has two main sub-types:

  • Episodic memory — memory for events you personally experienced, embedded in time and place: what you had for breakfast, the lecture where the professor dropped the chalk. Episodic memories are vivid, contextual, and fade quickly unless rehearsed or emotionally charged.
  • Semantic memory — general knowledge stripped of the context in which you learned it: the capital of France, what a derivative is, the meaning of "hippocampus". Most of what you learn in school is meant to become semantic memory. Semantic memories typically begin as episodic ones (you remember the moment you learned the fact) and become semantic through repeated retrieval in varied contexts, as systems consolidation strips away the episodic detail.

Declarative memory learns fast — a single exposure can be enough — but forgets fast too, and it is highly sensitive to the encoding and retrieval factors above.

Non-declarative (implicit) memory — knowing how#

  • Procedural memory — skills and habits: typing, riding a bicycle, playing a scale, pronouncing a foreign sound, solving a certain kind of equation fluently. Procedural memory depends on the basal ganglia and cerebellum more than the hippocampus. It learns slowly, through many repetitions with feedback, but what it learns is remarkably durable (you don't forget how to ride a bike). It is largely inaccessible to conscious report — experts often cannot explain how they do what they do — and it is not improved by rereading anything.
  • Priming and perceptual learning — the tuning of perception by exposure: learning to hear the difference between two phonemes, recognising a chess position or an X-ray pattern at a glance. This is the substrate of expert intuition.
  • Conditioning — learned associations between stimuli and responses, including emotional responses. Test anxiety is largely conditioned.

The difference between declarative and procedural memory explains a great deal:

  • Why you can know all the grammar rules of a language and still be unable to speak it (declarative knowledge without procedural fluency), and why immersion learners can speak fluently without being able to state a single rule (the reverse).
  • Why studying a maths textbook is not the same as doing problems.
  • Why a brilliant explanation, fully understood, does not make you able to do the thing.
  • Why skill learning needs a different schedule — more repetitions, spaced, with feedback — than fact learning.

Most real-world expertise requires both: declarative knowledge to understand why, and procedural fluency to do. A doctor needs to know the physiology (declarative) and to recognise a pattern of symptoms instantly (perceptual/procedural). A programmer needs to understand the language semantics and to produce syntax without conscious thought. Good learning plans train both, using different methods for each.

Working memory — the bottleneck#

Working memory is not long-term storage at all but the small, active workspace in which you hold and manipulate information right now: the number you're carrying in a mental calculation, the first half of a sentence while you read the second half. It has a capacity of roughly four "chunks" and a duration of a few seconds without rehearsal. Everything you learn passes through it, and its severe limits are the single biggest constraint on instruction and study design. It gets its own treatment in Chapter 4 and Chapter 5.

Understanding versus remembering versus being able to do#

Learners routinely confuse three things:

  1. Understanding — the experience of an explanation making sense while you're following it.
  2. Remembering — being able to recall the content later, without the explanation in front of you.
  3. Being able to do — applying it to a new problem, in a new context, under some pressure.

Each is necessary for the next, and none guarantees the next. Understanding in the moment is comprehension; it is real, but it is temporary and it does not automatically produce memory. Bjork's "learning versus performance" is precisely this gap: the lecture made sense (performance), and yet two weeks later you cannot reproduce the argument (no learning).

This is why the feeling "I get it" is such an unreliable guide. Comprehension monitoring — the feeling of understanding — is driven by fluency: how smoothly the information is processing right now. Fluency is high whenever the material is well presented, whenever you've just seen it, and whenever the answer is in front of you. None of those things predicts whether you'll be able to retrieve it in a month.

The only reliable test of learning is the one built into the definition: can you demonstrate it later, in a different context, without the material present? Every technique in Part II is, at bottom, a way of making your study time look more like that test.

The forgetting curve — and why it is your friend#

In the 1880s Hermann Ebbinghaus taught himself lists of nonsense syllables and measured how much he retained after various intervals. He found that forgetting is rapid at first and then slows: a large fraction is lost within the first day, but what survives the first few days is lost much more slowly. The resulting curve has been replicated many times, including in a direct 2015 replication by Murre and Dros.

Two things about the forgetting curve matter enormously:

First, the curve flattens with each successful relearning. If you relearn material after some forgetting has occurred, the new forgetting curve is shallower. Repeat this a few times and the curve becomes nearly flat: the memory is effectively permanent. This is the mechanism behind spaced repetition (Chapter 9).

Second, forgetting is not the enemy of learning — it is part of it. Bjork's "new theory of disuse" holds that some forgetting before you retrieve something makes the retrieval more effortful and therefore more powerful. Reviewing material you still know perfectly does almost nothing. Reviewing it when it has become hard to recall — but still possible — produces the largest gains. Forgetting creates the opportunity for the kind of effortful retrieval that builds durable memory.

So the goal is not to prevent forgetting. It is to retrieve at the right moments — after some forgetting, before too much.

Putting it together: a model of learning to carry with you#

Here is the mental model that the rest of the guide builds on:

  1. Attention selects what enters working memory. No attention, no learning.
  2. Working memory is a tiny workspace. Overload it and nothing gets encoded properly. Good instruction and good self-study manage this load.
  3. Encoding into long-term memory is strongest when you process for meaning, connect to prior knowledge, generate rather than receive, and make things distinctive.
  4. Consolidation happens over hours and days, largely in sleep, and can be disrupted by sleep loss and by interference.
  5. Retrieval is where learning shows itself and where it is most powerfully strengthened. Effortful, successful retrieval after some forgetting is the engine of durable memory.
  6. Different memory systems need different training. Facts and concepts (declarative) respond to retrieval, spacing and elaboration. Skills (procedural) respond to large amounts of varied, feedback-rich practice spread over time. Most real expertise needs both.
  7. You cannot feel learning; you can only feel performance and fluency. So you must test yourself and trust the tests, not the feeling.
Try this

Take something you "learned" in the past week — a concept from a lecture, a chapter you read, a talk you watched. Without looking at any notes, write down everything you can remember about it, as if explaining it to a friend. Then compare with the source. The gap between what you thought you knew and what you could actually produce is the gap between performance and learning. Most people find it sobering, and it is the best possible motivation for what follows.
